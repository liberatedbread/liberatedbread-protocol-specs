    Blueair Cabin P2i Car Air Purifier¶

    Status: Spec Available Protocol: BLE Manufacturer: Blueair AB (BLE stack and SDK by Airboxlab, the company behind Foobot) Manufacturer Status: Active (the product is controlled locally over BLE, but the vendor app wants an account and uploads readings)

    Overview¶

    The Cabin P2i is a 12 V purifier for a car. The purifier unit is strapped behind a front-seat headrest. It holds a Particle+Carbon filter and a laser particle sensor from the Plantower family. A 4.5 m cable runs from the unit to a control knob that sits in the car's 12 V socket. The knob powers the system, shows air quality and fan speed on a ring of LEDs, and has its own USB charging port. The unit has a second USB port. The manual lists a twin-unit Cabin P2iD beside it.

    Inside the Blueair app the product is called ICP. Its Bluetooth side is not Blueair's own work. The BLE SDK in the app was written by Airboxlab (Foobot), and so was the firmware update service it uses. That lineage explains the update-mode name "AirBoxLab" and why the firmware is hosted in a Foobot storage bucket.

    Everything a user can do from the app works locally over BLE with no cloud:

    • fan speed (low, medium, high)
    • fan mode (manual, auto)
    • live PM1 / PM2.5 / PM10 and six particle-count bins
    • filter life
    • a fault bitfield
    • a history of stored readings

    The cloud account is only where the app sends those readings.

    Out of scope. Blueair's Wi-Fi purifiers (Classic i-series, Sense+, Aware, Blue Signature, HealthProtect 74xx/77xx, Blue Pure / Blue Max 211i–511i) and its humidifiers and fans come through the same app, but none of them uses this protocol. The app onboards them with Espressif protocomm over BLE (advertised service 4772911e-d07c-4617-8241-f4d10948d6ae) or through a SoftAP, and then controls them through Blueair's cloud.

    None of this has been driven against hardware by this project, and no capture exists. Every byte below comes from static analysis of the Android app and of two of the vendor's BLE-module firmware packages, plus the vendor manual. The service layout, the security levels and the advertising data were read out of the module's own firmware, which is as close to a GATT dump as you get without the hardware, but still not one. In the spec it is all reported, and the spec is untested.

    Hardware¶

    Property Value
    Model Number Cabin P2i (single unit). Cabin P2iD (two units) appears in the manual's performance table; its BLE identity is not known
    Chipset Three processors. BLE module: Nordic nRF52. The DFU packages target hw_version 52 and SoftDevice S132 v5.0.0, and a 220 KB application beside S132 only fits an nRF52832, so that part number is an inference. Main MCU in the purifier unit: the firmware the update package carries for it is an Arm Cortex-M image linked at 0x08000000, the STM32 convention, so probably an STM32 or a clone (also an inference). Knob MCU, which the app and firmware call the "plug"
    Sensor Laser particle sensor, Plantower family (the BLE module's log strings name Plantower sensor errors). Exact model not established
    Radio BLE only (2.4 GHz). No Wi-Fi
    Power 12 V from the car's socket through the knob. The manual recommends an ignition-switched outlet
    FCC ID 2ARIK-P2I, grantee Blueair Tech (Shenzhen) Co., Ltd. Filed 2018-11-06, equipment class DTS, 2402–2480 MHz Bluetooth LE. Details are from the fccid.io listing as a search engine indexed it; the site refuses scripted requests, so the exhibits were not retrieved

    Initial Setup¶

    Property Value
    Setup required No. The only one-time step is the Bluetooth bond
    Method ble_direct
    Setup AP / advertised name Local name starting Blueair P2i (firmware 1.2.3 and 1.2.11) or Blueair ICP, matched case-insensitively as a prefix
    Passphrase protection not_applicable
    Confidence medium (decompiled app and vendor manual; not replayed here)

    To bring a unit up from a client:

    1. Fit the filter and the top cover. The unit refuses to start without the cover. Plug the knob into the 12 V socket and switch the ignition on.
    2. Flick the knob left or right once to turn the unit on. A blue LED on the top cover lights, and the fan resumes its last setting.
    3. Turn the knob clockwise and hold it for about 5 seconds. The knob's LEDs run a cycling animation, which is pairing mode. Any phone already connected is dropped.
    4. Scan for Blueair P2i / Blueair ICP and connect. The app scans for 30 s and takes the first match, not the strongest.
    5. Bond, and wait for the bond to complete.
    6. Request a larger ATT MTU. The app asks for 517. At least 41 is needed, because the stored-record notifications are 38 bytes.
    7. Discover services and run the connect sequence under Protocol Summary.

    Factory reset: not established. The BLE module erases every bond when the main MCU tells it to, and the main-MCU image inside the update package has a factory-reset routine of its own. No gesture in the manual or the app is known to reach either. Two things are easily mistaken for a reset:

    • The FILTER RESET button on the purifier base only restarts the filter counter. The module's logs say it resets it to 360 h.
    • Turning the knob anticlockwise and holding for 3 s only puts the unit into idle, with the lights off.

    Nobody has worked out what clears the bond list. For anyone adopting a second-hand unit, that is the open question.

    Rebinding to a new network: not applicable, since there is no network. To add a phone, repeat the knob pairing gesture. The unit keeps a bounded list of bonded phones, and its logs show it replacing an entry when the list is full.

    Pairing¶

    Property Value
    Pairing required Yes. The app always bonds before it touches a characteristic
    Security mode just_works (LE legacy pairing). From the module's firmware, not observed on air
    Bonding required
    PIN source None known
    One central at a time Unknown
    One bond at a time No. There is a bonded-peer list with a maximum size, which is not established
    Confidence medium (app flow, firmware log strings and the firmware's pairing parameters; the exchange itself was never observed)

    The firmware makes pairing mode matter more than usual. Once the unit holds any bond, the BLE module advertises only to whitelisted (bonded) phones. With an empty bond list and no pairing mode, it does not advertise at all. An unbonded client can therefore get in only while pairing mode is active.

    The firmware also settles how the pairing goes:

    • Pairing parameters. Bonding is on, I/O capabilities are none, and LE Secure Connections is off. Keys are 7 to 16 bytes, and encryption and identity keys are distributed both ways. The module also sets the MITM and OOB flags, but a phone has no OOB data for it, so LE legacy pairing falls back to Just Works with unauthenticated keys.
    • Encryption is required. Every custom characteristic value, and every CCCD, is registered with security mode 1 level 2: an encrypted link, with unauthenticated keys accepted. Without a bond, a read, write or subscribe is refused with an insufficient-encryption error, and most host stacks then pair on their own.
    • One exception. The factory AT-command service 0x1337 drops the requirement while the unit is in its factory test-rig mode.

    Entering pairing mode: with the unit on, turn the knob clockwise and hold it for about 5 seconds, until the knob's lights cycle. The main MCU tells the BLE module to enter pairing mode, and the module disconnects any current phone and advertises to everyone. Pairing mode ends when a bond completes or when a timeout runs out. The timeout's length was not recovered.

    Unpairing: no user-facing route is known. The firmware can erase all bonds when the main MCU asks, but nothing found here makes it ask. Forgetting the unit on a phone clears only the phone's half.

    Recovery:

    • If another phone holds the link, close the vendor app or switch Bluetooth off on that phone. Entering pairing mode from the knob also drops it.
    • If bonding fails after the unit lost its half of a bond, forget the unit in the phone's Bluetooth settings and repeat the knob gesture.

    Protocol Summary¶

    UUIDs: 16-bit shorts on the SIG base¶

    Every custom UUID here is a 16-bit short on the Bluetooth SIG base, 0000xxxx-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb, services and characteristics alike. The shorts are not SIG-assigned. The app builds its characteristic UUIDs that way and the module registers them that way, so write them exactly as expanded.

    The app never names a service. It runs discovery and then addresses each characteristic by its full UUID, and a client should do the same. The service layout below comes from the module's own firmware, where each service is registered as a primary service with a 16-bit UUID. It has not been seen in a GATT dump.

    BLE Services¶

    UUID Name Description
    00000001-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb Identity, clock and status Firmware versions, clock, fault flags, unit type, unit state (0x0001–0x0005). Always advertised
    00000002-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb Fan and air quality Fan speed and mode, run-time counters, stored records, live sensor frames (0x0011–0x0017). Advertised in normal operation
    00000003-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb Update progress 0x0060 only. Not advertised
    00001337-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb Factory AT-command channel 0x0040 / 0x0041, for the factory test rig. Leave it alone (see below)
    0000180a-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb Device Information (SIG) Serial, firmware and hardware revision, manufacturer
    0000fe59-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb Nordic Secure DFU Buttonless DFU entry

    Every characteristic in the four custom services carries a Characteristic User Description with a short English label, so a GATT explorer shows names. Every value is fixed-length in the module's table (the stack's variable-length flag is off), so write exactly the length shown below.

    Characteristics¶

    UUID Service Properties Length Role
    00002a25-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb 0x180a read text Serial number and BLE address, UTF-8 <serial>;<address>
    00002a26-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb 0x180a read text Firmware revision, UTF-8. The app replaces it with field 0 of 0x0001
    00002a27-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb 0x180a read text Hardware revision, UTF-8
    00002a29-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb 0x180a read text Manufacturer name, UTF-8

    00000011-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb 0x0002 read, notify 1 Fan speed reported (what the unit is running)

    00000017-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb 0x0002 notify 26 Live particle-sensor frames

    00000041-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fb 0x1337 notify 128 Factory AT command response
    8ec90003-f315-4f60-9fb8-838830daea50 0xfe59 write, indicate – Nordic buttonless DFU entry (without bond sharing)

    The properties and lengths of the custom characteristics come from the module's GATT table. They agree with how the app uses each one, and add three things the app never touches: 0x0002 is readable, 0x0004 is read/notify, and there are the 0x0005 and 0x1337 characteristics. The Device Information and DFU properties are the stack's defaults.

    Advertising¶

    The firmware builds its advertising data from these parts:

    • Flags 0x06: LE General Discoverable, BR/EDR not supported.
    • Name: Blueair P2i.
    • Appearance: included. The value was not traced.
    • Complete list of 16-bit service UUIDs: 0x0002, 0x0001 in normal operation. In the factory test-rig mode it is 0x0001, 0x1337 instead.

    It advertises fast, then slow. Whenever it holds bonds and is not in pairing mode, it filters by the whitelist. The spec leaves the service UUIDs out of its scan matcher on purpose. They come from static analysis, and 16-bit values this generic would pull in other hardware. Match on the name, as the app does.

    Factory AT-command service (0x1337)¶

    The module carries a text channel into an AT-style command interpreter, for the factory test rig. The same vocabulary runs its serial link to the main MCU, and it reaches fan control, the filter counters, the clock, bond deletion and the main-MCU and knob update steps.

    At boot the module enters a test-rig mode when its stored settings are uninitialised or its stored device ID is all zeros. In that mode the service is advertised and open. On a unit that has left the factory it is present but needs an encrypted link, like everything else.

    Several of those commands can wipe state or leave a board without firmware. None has been tried, and which of them this characteristic accepts was not traced, so the spec declares no commands for it. A client should not use it.

    Connect sequence¶

    This is the order the app uses after the bond, MTU request and service discovery:

    1. Read 0x0060 (update progress), then subscribe to it. While byte 0 is 2–5 the unit is still updating its main MCU or knob. The SDK can hold everything else until byte 0 reads 1 (not needed) or 6 (finished). The shipped app does not use that option, but a careful client should wait.
    2. Read 0x2a25, 0x2a26, 0x2a27, 0x2a29 and 0x0001, then 0x0003, 0x0011, 0x0013 and 0x0015.
    3. Write the phone's Unix time, as a uint32 LE, to 0x0002.
    4. Subscribe to 0x0003, 0x0011, 0x0013, 0x0015 and 0x0017.
    5. Optionally subscribe to 0x0016. The app does this only when a user is signed in, because it forwards the stored records to the cloud. It batches them in groups of up to 10 records or 10 s.

    Fan speed and mode¶

    A write goes to the requested characteristic. The BLE module passes it to the main MCU and then fetches the current value back, and the result arrives as a notification on the reported characteristic. The knob and auto mode change the same values, so render only the reported characteristic.

    Code Fan speed (0x0011 / 0x0012) Fan mode (0x0013 / 0x0014)
    0 Defined, meaning unknown. The app never writes it and displays a reported 0 as low Unknown
    1 Low Manual (the speed on 0x0012 applies)
    2 Medium Auto (the unit picks the speed from its sensor)
    3 High Max. Defined, but the app never writes it and the knob has no such position

    The app writes only speeds 1–3 and modes 1–2, and drops any reported value that is not exactly one byte in the range 0–3. The spec offers speed 0 and mode 3 only as advanced commands, because nobody has seen what the unit does with them.

    Fault flags (0x0003, and bytes 8–11 of every stored record)¶

    Four bytes, one per board. All zero means no fault.

    Byte Bit Meaning
    0 (purifier) 0x01 Top cover open or filter not seated. The unit will not run
    0 0x02 Button fault
    0 0x04 Battery fault
    0 0x08 Particle sensor not responding
    0 0x10 Particle sensor out of range
    0 0x20 Malfunction
    1 (knob) 0x01 Knob not responding. Probably the manual's "disconnected from the control plug"
    1 0x02 LED driver fault
    1 0x04 Buttons fault
    1 0x08 Malfunction
    1 0x10 USB overload. The manual says the system shuts down until the load is removed
    2 (BLE module) 0x01 Lost contact with the main MCU
    3 (second unit, Duo) 0x01 Top cover open
    3 0x02 Malfunction

    Unit state (0x0005, 1 byte, firmware 1.2.11 and later)¶

    The main MCU's operating state, which the BLE module keeps and notifies when it changes. Firmware 1.2.3 does not have this characteristic, and the app never uses it. The names are paraphrases of the firmware's own state-name table.

    Value State
    0 Unknown
    1 Powered off
    2 Starting up
    3 Filter due
    4 Normal
    5 Going to standby
    6 Standby
    7 Pairing mode
    8 Error
    9 Firmware update

    Run-time counters (0x0015, 28 bytes)¶

    Seven 32-bit little-endian counters, all in minutes. The app decodes each one as signed.

    Offset Field
    0 Minutes at speed 0 (meaning of "speed 0" not established)
    4 Minutes at low
    8 Minutes at medium
    12 Minutes at high
    16 Total fan-on minutes
    20 Live minutes (probably total powered time)
    24 Filter time remaining. 21600 (360 h) after a filter reset

    The manual rates a filter at about 360 hours of running, which it describes as about 6 months at 2.5 h a day. The app shows filter life as remaining × 100 / 21600, truncated to a whole percent and shown as 0 when remaining is 0 or less.

    Stored record (0x0016, 38 bytes, all little-endian)¶

    Offset Length Field
    0 4 Timestamp, Unix seconds, from the clock set through 0x0002
    4 4 Record number in the unit's data store
    8 4 Fault flags (the 0x0003 layout)
    12 2 PM1, µg/m³
    14 2 PM2.5, µg/m³
    16 2 PM10, µg/m³
    18 2 PM1, second figure
    20 2 PM2.5, second figure
    22 2 PM10, second figure
    24 2 Count > 0.3 µm
    26 2 Count > 0.5 µm
    28 2 Count > 1 µm
    30 2 Count > 2.5 µm
    32 2 Count > 5 µm
    34 2 Count > 10 µm
    36 1 Fan speed (signed byte, the 0x0011 codes)
    37 1 Fan mode (signed byte, the 0x0013 codes)

    The app drops any record that is not exactly 38 bytes.

    Live sensor frame (0x0017, 26 bytes)¶

    Offset Length Field
    0 1 Sensor status, signed. 0 = ok, −100 = CRC error, −102 = timeout; anything else is unknown
    1 1 Not read by the app
    2–25 12 × 2 The same twelve uint16 LE values as bytes 12–35 of the stored record

    The twelve values follow the order of the data words in a Plantower PMS frame. If Plantower's convention holds, the first PM set is the "CF=1" figure, the second is the "atmospheric environment" figure, and the counts are per 0.1 L of air. That is a hypothesis. The frame carries no timestamp, and the app shows the first PM set stamped with the phone's own clock.

    Update progress (0x0060, 9 bytes)¶

    Byte Meaning
    0 Step: 0 not started, 1 not needed, 2 BLE module updating, 3 main MCU updating, 4 copying the knob image to the main MCU, 5 knob updating, 6 finished, 7 enum bound
    1 / 2 BLE module: status, percent
    3 / 4 Main MCU: status, percent
    5 / 6 Knob image copy: status, percent
    7 / 8 Knob: status, percent

    The status codes are 0 unknown, 1 not needed, 2 in progress, 3 finished and 4 enum bound. The app treats the unit as settled when the step is 1 or 6, or when the knob stage reads finished.

    Firmware update¶

    The update uses Nordic Secure DFU. The app hands the unit's address and a package to the Nordic Android DFU library, with keep-bond set. The library enters the bootloader through the buttonless characteristic 8ec90003-f315-4f60-9fb8-838830daea50, and the bootloader comes up at the unit's address + 1. In update mode the unit advertises a name starting AirBoxLab. The app also accepts a name starting Dfu, which is generic, so tie it to this product only while an update you started is in progress.

    The cloud tells the app where the package is. The app fetches it over plain HTTP from firmware.foobot.io, a publicly listable Foobot/Airboxlab bucket that also holds Blueair's other firmware. The two packages read for this spec (application 1.2.3 and 1.2.11) are application-only and need S132 v5.0.0. They carry an ECDSA P-256 signed init packet, so a replacement client can deliver the vendor's packages but cannot build its own. Both packages declare application version 0, so the bootloader's version check will not tell releases apart.

    The BLE module's application image carries the main MCU's firmware inside it: there are embedded Arm Cortex-M images with vector tables linked at 0x08000000. Where the knob's image sits was not traced. After the BLE module reboots, the unit carries on by itself. It pushes new firmware to the main MCU and then to the knob, reporting each stage on 0x0060, and the knob glows green meanwhile. The manual says not to power the unit off until it restarts. The spec marks the bootloader entry as advanced and reproduces no image or init-packet bytes.

    The app has a separate path for resuming an update on a unit it finds already in update mode. By flash arithmetic, a 220 KB application beside S132 on an nRF52832 leaves no room for a second bank. An interrupted transfer therefore probably leaves the unit in its bootloader. Getting it out needs a Nordic DFU client and the vendor package.

    Troubleshooting¶

    • Scan finds nothing. The unit advertises only to phones it has bonded with, and not at all when it has no bonds. Put it into pairing mode with the knob (clockwise, about 5 s). Also check that the 12 V socket is live with the ignition on and that the top cover is seated.
    • Bonding fails or the link drops right after connecting. Pairing mode was not active or had timed out. Repeat the gesture. If the phone remembers an old bond, forget the unit first.
    • Reads or subscriptions fail with an insufficient-encryption (or authentication) error. Every custom characteristic needs an encrypted link. Bond first; most stacks start pairing on this error by themselves, but some need it asked for explicitly.
    • Stored-record or counter notifications are cut short. The MTU was not raised. At the default MTU a notification carries 20 bytes, and the app drops anything that is not exactly 38 or 28 bytes.
    • Filter life never updates after a filter change. The counter resets only from the FILTER RESET button on the purifier base. No BLE command for it is known.

    Defects found in the vendor app, which a replacement client should not copy:

    • The fault-flag parser accepts a 3-byte value and then reads a fourth byte, which throws.
    • The live-frame parser has no length check. A short frame throws and ends the stream until the next connection.
    • The live-frame status table includes −200 (sensor not initialised), which the signed byte it reads cannot represent.
    • Onboarding takes the first matching advertiser within its 30 s scan. With two units in pairing mode it can bond the wrong one.
    • Stored records are collected only while a user is signed in, because they go to the cloud. A phone used only locally never drains them.
    • Resuming an interrupted update looks the unit up in the cloud account's device list. Without the cloud, a stuck unit needs a generic Nordic DFU tool and the package.
